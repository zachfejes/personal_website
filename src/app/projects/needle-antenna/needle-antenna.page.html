A needle, driven

A thin metal needle floats in empty space, driven at its base by an ideal AC voltage source whose other terminal meets an identical mirror needle: together they make a center-fed dipole antenna. The needle behaves as a waveguide: current and charge settle into a standing wave along it, the wave leaks into the space around it as radiation, and the metal turns part of it into heat. Change the needle, the drive and the material, and watch the steady state follow.

03 — Heat

The current crowds into a skin a few micrometers deep, and that thin skin’s resistance turns ½|I|²R′ watts per meter into heat, most of it where the standing wave’s current peaks. In vacuum there is no convection: heat spreads along the metal by conduction and leaves by thermal radiation from the surface, plus, if the feed is heat-sunk, by conduction out through the base. The mirror needle heats identically.

05 — How it’s modeled

  1. Circuit. The antenna floats in free space. The ideal source sits in a vanishingly small gap between the needle’s base and an identical mirror needle, through its series feed impedance, with any gap capacitance across it. By symmetry each needle sits at ±½ the gap voltage relative to the midplane, and the same current flows up both.
  2. Geometry. The needle is a cylinder, then a cone, then a spherical cap that the cone meets tangentially, so the radius a(z) and its slope are continuous all the way to the apex. Every quantity below is computed from this exact profile.
  3. Needle as a waveguide. Each needle is a non-uniform transmission line against the midplane (Schelkunoff’s thin-dipole model) whose distributed parameters vary continuously with a(z): characteristic impedance Z₀(z) = (η/2π)(ln(2L/a(z)) − 1), so the point raises it; series impedance jωL′ plus the metal’s internal impedance (skin-effect resistance and internal inductance, blended from the DC and thin-skin limits); shunt admittance jωC′. The telegrapher’s equations dV/dz = −Z′(z)I, dI/dz = −Y′(z)V are integrated as differential equations (fourth-order Runge–Kutta, coefficients evaluated at the exact radius at every stage) from the apex, loaded by the cap’s capacitance, back to the gap. Between nodes, V and I are cubic Hermite interpolants whose slopes come from the same equations.
  4. Radiation. Energy radiated is a loss the line does not know about, so it is added as a uniform series resistance and iterated: solve the line, compute the power the current actually radiates from its far field, update the resistance, until they agree ({{ sol().convergence.iterations }} iterations here{{ r.converged ? '' : ', not fully converged' }}).
  5. Fields. Charge follows from current by continuity, λ = C′V. The fields are integrals of the exact retarded fields of the current and charge along both needles (Gauss–Legendre quadrature), E = −jωA − ∇Φ and H = ∇×A/μ, with a thin-wire kernel that keeps them finite at the surface. Nothing is near- or far-field approximated, so the reactive near field, the induction zone and the radiation zone all appear.
  6. Heat. Time-averaged Joule heat ½|I|²R′ per meter, integrated along the needle. The steady temperature solves one-dimensional conduction along the needle, with conductances and radiating areas taken from the exact profile, and grey-body radiation εσ(T⁴ − T₀⁴) from the surface. The base is insulated (a floating antenna) or held at ambient (a heat-sunk feed). Material properties stay at room-temperature values.
  7. Limits. The line model assumes the needle is thin (radius ≪ length and wavelength). Fields within a few radii of the surface and at the apex are approximate, the feed gap is idealized as infinitely small, and the medium is vacuum. Field emission and breakdown at very high tip fields are not modeled.
