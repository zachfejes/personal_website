Simulation · Electromagnetics

A needle, driven

A thin metal needle floats in empty space: a straight wire, pointed at one end and rounded at the other, cut by a tiny gap where an ideal AC voltage source drives it (through a balun, so the feed is balanced). The two pieces either side of the gap make a dipole antenna, fed at its center or anywhere along its length. The needle behaves as a waveguide: current and charge settle into a standing wave along it, the wave leaks into the space around it as radiation, and the metal turns part of it into heat. Change the needle, the drive and the material, and watch the steady state follow.

03 — Heat

The current crowds into a skin a few micrometers deep, and that thin skin’s resistance turns ½|I|²R′ watts per meter into heat, most of it where the standing wave’s current peaks. Heat spreads along the metal by conduction and leaves by thermal radiation and, outside vacuum, by convection into the air, oil or water around each stretch (liquids carry it away far better than air), plus through the feed if it is heat-sunk. The charts run from the rounded end (left) to the point (right); shaded bands mark the stretches in oil and water.

05 — How it’s modeled

  1. Circuit. The antenna floats in free space. The ideal source, behind a balun (so no current runs on the outside of the feed line), sits in a vanishingly small gap that splits the wire into the needle arm (gap to point) and the other arm (gap to rounded end), through its series feed impedance, with any gap capacitance across it. The gap can sit anywhere along the wire. The same current I flows through the gap and into both arms, so the arms are in series: V_gap = I·(Z_needle + Z_other), each arm’s impedance solved on its own line. Moving the gap off center changes the arms’ lengths, and so where the feed sits on the standing wave: toward a current minimum, the input resistance rises.
  2. Surroundings. Air at 1 atm (εr = 1.0006) above a flat oil layer of set thickness and permittivity, floating on deep deionized water. The water’s permittivity follows a Debye relaxation, ε = ε∞ + (εs − ε∞)/(1 + jωτ), with the static permittivity of Malmberg and Maryott and the relaxation time of Stogryn, both at the surroundings’ temperature (about 78.7 − j10.3 at 2.4 GHz and 20 °C). The antenna lies in a vertical plane, its point tilted down through the oil into the water.
  3. Geometry. The needle is a cylinder, then a cone, then a spherical cap that the cone meets tangentially, so the radius a(z) and its slope are continuous all the way to the apex. The other arm is a cylinder ending in a hemisphere of the shaft radius (or, optionally, a copy of the needle). Every quantity below is computed from these exact profiles.
  4. Needle as a waveguide. Each arm is a non-uniform transmission line against the midplane (Schelkunoff’s thin-dipole model) whose distributed parameters vary continuously with a(z): characteristic impedance Z₀(z) = (η/2π)(ln(2L/a(z)) − 1), with L half the total length (shared by both arms wherever the feed is), so the point raises it; series impedance jωL′ plus the metal’s internal impedance (skin-effect resistance and internal inductance, blended from the DC and thin-skin limits); shunt admittance jωC′·εr from the medium around each stretch, so oil and water slow the wave and water’s loss (ε″) becomes a shunt conductance that heats it. Mesh nodes sit exactly where the wire crosses each surface. The telegrapher’s equations dV/dz = −Z′(z)I, dI/dz = −Y′(z)V are integrated as differential equations (fourth-order Runge–Kutta, coefficients evaluated at the exact radius at every stage) from the apex, loaded by the cap’s capacitance, back to the gap. Between nodes, V and I are cubic Hermite interpolants whose slopes come from the same equations.
  5. Radiation. Energy radiated is a loss the line does not know about, so it is added as a series resistance (scaled at each point by the local refractive index, as a small dipole’s radiation is in a medium) and iterated: solve the line, compute the power the current actually radiates from its far field, update the resistance, until they agree ({{ sol().convergence.iterations }} iterations here{{ r.converged ? '' : ', not fully converged' }}).
  6. Fields. Charge follows from current by continuity, λ = C′εr·V. The fields are sums of the retarded fields of the current and charge along both arms, E = −jωA − ∇Φ and H = ∇×A/μ, with a thin-wire kernel that keeps them finite at the surface. In uniform surroundings that is exact for the given currents, near field and far. With layers, each source uses its own layer’s complex wavenumber and permittivity, plus a quasi-static image in each surface bounding that layer, weighted by (ε₁ − ε₂)/(ε₁ + ε₂). Fields in other layers take the phase and attenuation accumulated along the straight path through each layer, and the charge carried across each surface by 2ε₂/(ε₁ + ε₂). Heating in the liquids is ½ωε₀ε″|E|². The plane shown contains the antenna and the surface normal, where E lies in the plane and H crosses it.
  7. Heat. Time-averaged Joule heat ½|I|²R′ per meter, integrated along the needle. The steady temperature solves one-dimensional conduction along the needle, with conductances and radiating areas taken from the exact profile, and grey-body radiation εσ(T⁴ − T₀⁴) from the surface. The base is insulated (a floating antenna) or held at ambient (a heat-sunk feed). Material properties stay at room-temperature values.
  8. Limits. The line model assumes the needle is thin (radius ≪ length and wavelength). Fields within a few radii of the surface and at the apex are approximate, the feed gap is idealized as infinitely small. In the layers, the line model treats each stretch of wire as if surrounded by its own medium, radiation from each stretch is scaled by the local refractive index from the vacuum far field, and the fields use quasi-static images and transmission factors at the surfaces, so they are approximate within about a wavelength of the oil. Field emission and breakdown at very high tip fields are not modeled.
